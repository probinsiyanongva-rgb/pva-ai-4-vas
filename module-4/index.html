AI for Research, Summarizing & Information

The question changes here. Modules 1–3 asked "how can AI help me communicate this?" This module asks: how do you use AI to work with information without blindly trusting it? The discipline is simple — AI claim → source → verify → use.

As before, picking an answer in a practice exercise marks it done in your progress — it isn't graded. The goal is judgment, not a score.

4.1 VA Skill: Research preparation

AI as a Research Assistant

Big Question How can AI help you research without becoming the source you blindly trust?
Learning Objective

Create a practical research plan using AI to develop research questions and search directions while distinguishing AI assistance from information that still needs to be sourced and verified.

1. Why This Matters to a VA

A client might ask you to research competitors, find potential software, compare service providers, investigate an industry, or gather information for a decision. The difficult part is often not finding information — it's knowing what you actually need to find, where to look, which questions matter, and which claims need verification. AI can help you prepare for research. That is different from treating AI's answer as your research.

2. The VA Task Comes First

"We're thinking about improving our student onboarding process. Can you research what other online learning companies are doing for new-student onboarding and give me some ideas we can consider?"

Still too broad to research effectively. Before searching, define what "onboarding" means for this assignment — possible areas: welcome communication, account setup, orientation, first-week guidance, support resources, reminders, progress tracking. The VA needs to turn a broad request into researchable questions.

3. Where AI Fits

AI can help brainstorm research questions, identify areas worth investigating, suggest search terms, organize a research plan, help define comparison criteria, and identify gaps. AI should not automatically be treated as the factual source for the research.

Important distinctionAI can help you decide "what should I investigate?" That does not mean AI has already established "what is true?" The second question requires sources.

4. Research Questions vs. Research Findings

Research question: "What types of communication do online learning companies use during the first week after enrollment?" — something you can investigate. Research finding: "Most online learning companies send three emails during the first week." — a factual claim that requires evidence. Do not turn an AI-generated statement into a research finding simply because it sounds reasonable.

5. What to Give AI

Task: Prepare a research plan for improving BrightPath's student onboarding.

Context: Sarah wants ideas based on how other online learning companies approach new-student onboarding.

Research goal: Identify useful onboarding practices BrightPath could consider.

Focus areas: Welcome communication, orientation, first-week guidance, support resources, reminders, progress guidance.

Output: Research questions, search terms, suggested source types, and a simple comparison structure.

Constraint: Do not present AI-generated assumptions as research findings.

6. What to Ask AI to Do

"Help me turn this client request into a research plan. Create research questions, useful search terms, and categories for organizing findings. Focus on information that could help compare onboarding practices. Do not provide unsupported factual claims as findings."

This uses AI for planning rather than outsourcing the research judgment.

7. Demonstration

Weak approach: ask AI "What are the best onboarding practices used by online learning companies?" and copy the answer into a client report. Problem: the output may contain unchecked claims.

Better approach: use AI to produce research questions (e.g. "What information do learning companies provide immediately after enrollment?", "What communication channels do they use?") and search directions (e.g. "online learning student onboarding," "learning platform new student orientation"). Now the VA can conduct research against actual sources.

8. Research Assistance Is Not Factual Authority

Think of AI as a research assistant sitting beside you. It can say "you may want to investigate this," help you organize what you find, and help you compare information. But when the client asks "where did this information come from?" — you need a source.

One thing worth being precise about: if AI compares pieces of information you've already gathered, that comparison only checks whether they're consistent with each other — it doesn't independently verify that any of them are actually true. Confirming truth still requires going back to a source (this is the focus of Lesson 4.4).

9. PRACTICE: Improve the Research Request LEARNING ARTIFACT

"Can you research some tools we could use to improve our customer support?"

Turn this into a clearer research goal, five research questions, useful search terms, and comparison criteria (e.g. features, ease of use, integrations, pricing, support, suitability for BrightPath — don't assume every criterion is equally important; the client goal should determine what matters).

10. PRACTICE: What Is the AI Doing?

For each activity, decide whether AI is being used for research planning, research information, or source verification.

11. PROVE: Research Plan PROOF-OF-SKILL ARTIFACT

12. AI-Assisted Practice

Use AI to help create your research plan, then review it: are the questions actually useful, are any too broad, are search terms specific enough, are source types appropriate, did AI introduce assumptions about what BrightPath needs, what did you change?

13. Short AI Audit Note

14. Human Judgment Checkpoint

If AI disappeared right now, would I still know what I'm trying to find and why? If yes, AI has helped prepare the research. If no, the plan is too dependent on AI output.

15. Reflection

16. Lesson Close

AI can help you start research faster, ask better questions, and organize the work. But research does not end when AI gives you an answer — it starts with knowing what you need to find. Next: turning a long source into a useful summary.

4.2 VA Skill: Information summarization

Summarizing Long Information

Big Question How do you turn a long document into a useful summary without losing what matters?
Learning Objective

Create a client-facing summary from a long source document, using AI to organize and condense the material while checking the summary against the original source for accuracy, completeness, and context.

1. Why This Matters to a VA

Clients often don't have time to read everything — meeting transcripts, long email threads, reports, articles, process documents. "Can you summarize this for me?" sounds simple. It isn't. A bad summary can leave out an important decision, confuse an action item, change the meaning of a statement, turn a possibility into a fact, remove important context, or make an unresolved issue sound resolved. AI is particularly useful for handling large amounts of text — the VA is still responsible for deciding whether the summary is accurate and useful.

2. The VA Task Comes First

"I don't have time to read the whole transcript. Can you give me a summary with the main decisions, action items, and anything that still needs my attention?"

The transcript contains discussion of the onboarding process, ideas discussed but not approved, two decisions agreed upon, tasks assigned to Sarah, tasks assigned to Daniel, one unresolved question, and side discussions. Your job is not to make the transcript shorter — it's to make the important information easier for Sarah to use.

3. Where AI Fits

AI can identify major topics, extract decisions, find action items, group related information, create an initial summary, reduce repetition, and organize a long document. The VA needs to understand the client's requested summary, determine what matters, distinguish decisions from ideas, distinguish assigned tasks from suggestions, preserve unresolved issues, check the summary against the source, and remove unsupported conclusions.

4. Summarization Has a Purpose

There's no single "best summary" — it depends on what the reader needs. "What decisions were made?" → prioritize decisions. "What do I need to do next?" → prioritize action items. "Give me the main points" → a broader overview.

Key principleSummarize for the reader's purpose. Do not simply ask AI to "summarize this" and accept whatever comes back.

5. What to Give AI

Task: Summarize a BrightPath operations meeting transcript.

Context: Sarah needs a quick overview instead of reading the full transcript.

Purpose: Help Sarah understand the main decisions, action items, and unresolved issues.

Audience: Sarah, operations manager.

Required sections: Key decisions, Action items, Open questions, Important context.

Constraint: Use only information in the transcript. Do not treat ideas discussed during the meeting as approved decisions.

6. What to Ask AI to Do

"Summarize this meeting transcript for Sarah. Organize the summary into Key Decisions, Action Items, Open Questions, and Important Context. Use only information from the transcript. Distinguish decisions that were actually agreed upon from ideas or possibilities that were discussed but not approved. Do not invent owners, deadlines, or decisions."

7. Demonstration

Source: "Sarah: I think we should add a welcome video to the onboarding sequence. Daniel: That could work, I'll check what would be involved. Sarah: Let's hold off for now — we need to review the current onboarding emails first. Daniel: I'll update the onboarding email draft by Thursday. Sarah: Good, we'll review that version before deciding whether we need anything else."

"BrightPath decided to add a welcome video and Daniel will update the onboarding process."

The summary turned an idea into a decision and changed the scope of Daniel's task.

Key Decision — The team will review the current onboarding emails before deciding whether to add a welcome video.
Action Item — Daniel will update the onboarding email draft by Thursday.
Open Question — Whether a welcome video should be added remains undecided.

This preserves the actual meaning.

8. AI Output Audit

Four checks: Accuracy (does it match the source?), Completeness (did it preserve relevant information?), Context (discussed vs. decided, proposed vs. approved, possible vs. confirmed), Unsupported additions (did AI invent owners, deadlines, decisions, reasons, conclusions?).

9. Summarization Is Not Copying Everything

A summary should reduce the information the reader needs to process — but not by removing anything that seems inconvenient. "The team discussed three possible solutions but did not make a final decision" may be more important than listing all three. The summary should help the reader understand the status of the work.

10. PRACTICE: What Should Stay? LEARNING ARTIFACT

Source: Sarah wants to reduce the number of onboarding emails; Daniel suggested combining the second and third; Sarah agreed the sequence is too long; no final decision was made about combining; Daniel will prepare a revised sequence for review, expected Friday; Sarah asked the team to check whether students are missing the orientation link; Maya will review five recent support cases; the team will discuss findings next week.

Be careful — the transcript contains ideas, agreements, and tasks. They are not all the same thing.

11. PRACTICE: Find the Summary Error

Source: "Maya will review five recent support cases and report what she finds next week." AI summary: "Maya will review support cases and recommend changes to the onboarding process next week."

The source says Maya will review cases and report findings — it does not say she'll recommend changes. The AI summary introduced an unsupported responsibility.

12. PRACTICE: Summarize for a Different Reader

Use the same meeting information. Create two versions: for Sarah ("what needs my attention?") and for Daniel ("what do I need to work on?"). The source stays the same — the summary changes because the reader's purpose changes.

13. PROVE: Client-Facing Summary PROOF-OF-SKILL ARTIFACT

14. AI-Assisted Workflow

1. Understand

What does the client actually need from the summary?

2. Prepare

Identify the source material and relevant context.

3. Define

Decide what sections the summary needs.

4. Use AI

Ask AI to create an initial summary.

5. Audit

Compare the summary against the source.

6. Correct

Fix omissions, inaccurate wording, or misleading simplifications.

7. Deliver

Format the final summary for the intended reader.

15. Human Judgment Checkpoint

Could the client make a different decision because of something this summary says? If yes, carefully verify that information against the source. Did the summary preserve the difference between what was discussed, what was decided, and what remains unresolved?

16. Short AI Audit Note

17. Reflection

18. Lesson Close

Summarization is not simply making something shorter — it's deciding what the reader needs to know while staying faithful to the source. AI can process a large amount of text quickly. You are responsible for making sure the shorter version still means what the original meant. Next: extracting and organizing specific information from messy source material.

4.3 VA Skill: Information extraction

Extracting and Organizing Information

Big Question How do you turn scattered information into something you can actually work with?

1. Why This Matters to a VA

A lot of VA work starts with information that's difficult to use — a long email thread, meeting notes, customer messages, a transcript, a collection of notes. The client usually doesn't want the information copied back to them — they want something useful. "Can you pull out the action items and deadlines?" or "Can you organize the customer issues from these messages?" is where information extraction becomes useful. AI can help identify and organize information quickly — the VA still has to determine what matters, what belongs in the output, whether it's accurate, and whether anything was missed or invented.

2. The VA Task Comes First

"Can you organize the feedback so I can see the issue, who reported it, what part of onboarding it affects, and whether we need to follow up?"

The source material is messy — some notes are complete sentences, others short comments. Your job is not simply to summarize everything — it's to extract specific information and put it into a structure Sarah can use.

FieldWhat it means
IssueWhat problem or feedback was reported
SourceWhere the information came from
Onboarding AreaWhich part of onboarding it relates to
Follow-Up NeededWhether additional action or investigation is needed
NotesImportant context that should not be lost

3. Extraction Is Different From Summarization

Summarization asks: "What are the most important things in this material?" Extraction asks: "Which specific pieces of information do I need to pull out?"

Source: "Several new students said they were unsure where to find the orientation video. Maria mentioned that the link in the welcome email opens correctly, but she had to search through the email to find it. Daniel suggested making the link more visible."

A summary might say: "Several students had difficulty locating the orientation video in the welcome email." An extraction produces a structured table:

PersonIssueSuggested Action
Several studentsOrientation video difficult to findReview visibility of link
MariaHad to search the email for the linkConsider making link more visible
DanielSuggested making the link more visibleReview welcome email layout

The second output is more structured because the task requires specific information.

4. What Can You Extract?

Depending on the task: names, dates, deadlines, owners, action items, issues, requests, questions, decisions, product names, categories, contact information, status, amounts, requirements, repeated themes. The important question is: what information does the client actually need organized? Do not extract everything simply because AI can.

5. AI Can Help With Extraction

AI can locate repeated information, pull out specific fields, group similar items, convert notes into tables, identify action items, and organize by category. But AI can also miss information, misunderstand what belongs in a field, combine separate items, assign the wrong person, invent information to complete a table, or turn an implication into a fact. The extraction still needs review.

6. What to Give AI

Task: what should be extracted? Source Information: what material should AI work from? Output Structure: what fields or categories? Requirements: what should be included or excluded?

"Extract the issue, source, onboarding area, follow-up needed, and relevant notes from the material below. Use only information contained in the source. If a field cannot be determined from the source, leave it blank or mark it 'Not stated.' Do not infer an owner or action that is not stated."

A blank field is better than an invented answer.

7. PRACTICE: What Should Be Extracted? LEARNING ARTIFACT

"Please go through these student comments and organize the issues. I mainly want to know what the problem is, which part of onboarding it affects, and whether someone needs to look into it."

Which of these fields should be extracted? Issue, Student's favorite course, Onboarding area, Follow-up needed, Student's writing style, Source, Student's age, Relevant notes.

The strongest fields are Issue, Onboarding area, Follow-up needed, Source, and Relevant notes. The student's favorite course, writing style, and age aren't relevant unless Sarah specifically requested them. Extract for the purpose of the task.

8. PRACTICE: Find the Extraction Risk

Source: "John said the welcome email was clear, but he couldn't find the orientation video. Ana also had trouble locating the video. Mark suggested adding the video link near the top of the email."

PersonIssueAction
JohnCould not access the orientation videoFix broken video link
AnaCould not find the orientation videoFix broken video link
MarkSuggested moving the video linkMove the video link
The source says John and Ana had difficulty finding the video — it does not say the link was broken. AI converted a visibility problem into a technical problem. The VA should correct that.

Sarah: "Two students said the orientation video was difficult to find in the welcome email." Maria: "The link works, but it is near the bottom of the email." Daniel: "The FAQ page does not mention where the orientation video is located." Sarah: "Please check whether the welcome email can make the video easier to find." 10. PROVE: Structured Information Sheet PROOF-OF-SKILL ARTIFACT

Fields: Issue, Source, Category or area, Follow-up needed, Relevant notes.

Use only information supported by the source. Do not invent missing details. Keep separate issues separate when necessary. Combine duplicate information only when doing so does not remove useful context. Use clear, consistent wording. Make it useful to someone who did not read the original notes.

11. Human Judgment Checkpoint

  • Did I extract what the client actually asked for?
  • Did I accidentally turn an assumption into a fact?
  • Did AI combine information that should remain separate?
  • Did I leave anything important out?
  • Would Sarah understand the situation without reading all the original notes?

12. Short AI Audit Note

13. Reflection

14. Lesson Close

Extracting information is not the same as copying information. A good VA knows what the client needs, pulls out the relevant information, organizes it clearly, and checks that the structure still reflects the source. AI can make the extraction faster. You are still responsible for what the final table says.

4.4 VA Skill: Verification

Verify Before You Use It

Big Question How do you know whether information is reliable enough to use?

1. Why Verification Is Part of the VA Job

AI can make research work faster. It can also give you an answer that looks completely reasonable and is still wrong. If you pass incorrect information to a client, the client experiences it as incorrect work from their VA. The core habit: AI claim → source → verify → use.

2. What Should You Verify?

Pay particular attention to information that could affect a client decision or deliverable: dates, deadlines, prices, percentages, quantities, names, policies, requirements, current information, business facts, quoted information, claims about competitors, information used to make recommendations. A useful question: if this information were wrong, would it matter? If yes, verify it.

3. Primary vs. Secondary Sources

Primary source — directly provides the information (official company website, official policy, original report, government publication, client's own document). Secondary source — reports, summarizes, or interprets from elsewhere (news article, industry blog, review site, third-party summary). Secondary sources can be useful, but for important claims a primary source may provide stronger support when one is available.

4. AI Is Not the Final Source

AI can identify a claim, suggest what to investigate, suggest search terms, organize information, compare information you've already collected, and identify questions that need answering. AI should not automatically become the evidence for a client-facing factual claim.

Think of it this wayAI can point you toward information. The source supports the claim.

5. What to Check

Check the date (is it current?), the number (does it match the source?), the name (correctly identified?), the wording (does the source actually support what AI says?), the context (did AI leave out something that changes the meaning?), and the source itself (appropriate for the claim?).

6. PRACTICE: Spot the Verification Risk LEARNING ARTIFACT

Which of these claims should you verify before using them in a client deliverable?

  1. "BrightPath's current onboarding process includes a welcome video."
  2. "The company reduced its subscription price by 20% last year."
  3. "The new policy became effective on March 1."
  4. "Customers can request a refund within 30 days."
  5. "The report was published in 2025."
All five may require verification if being used as factual claims. The reason isn't that AI is always wrong — it's that the VA needs evidence before presenting important factual information as fact.

7. PRACTICE: Deliberate Risk

Source: "The competitor's standard plan is listed at $49 per month. The pricing page was checked on September 5, 2026." AI produces: "The competitor's standard plan costs $39 per month, according to its pricing page."

Do not use the AI-generated price. Check the actual pricing source — the source provided says $49, so the AI output conflicts with it. The goal is practicing catching an error that could affect a client-facing comparison.

8. PRACTICE: Date Verification

Source: "The updated onboarding guide was published on August 28, 2026." AI writes: "...published on August 18, 2026."

The date needs to be checked against the original source. The difference is only ten days, but that doesn't make it harmless — a small factual error is still an error.

9. PRACTICE: Scope Inflation

Source: "Five students reported difficulty locating the orientation video in the welcome email." AI writes: "The onboarding process is confusing and is causing students to abandon their onboarding."

No. The source supports one specific finding — five students had difficulty locating the video. It does not establish that the entire process is confusing or that students are abandoning onboarding. AI expanded the scope of the evidence beyond what the source establishes. Remove or qualify the unsupported claim.

10. PROVE: Build a Verification Table PROOF-OF-SKILL ARTIFACT

Four claims from an AI-assisted research task:

  1. Claim: "The competitor's standard plan costs $39 per month." Source: the pricing page lists it at $49.
  2. Claim: "The updated onboarding guide was published on August 18, 2026." Source: the guide states August 28, 2026.
  3. Claim: "Five students reported difficulty locating the orientation video." Source: Sarah reported five student comments describing this.
  4. Claim: "Students are abandoning onboarding because they cannot find the orientation video." Source: five students reported difficulty; no abandonment data was provided.

Build a table with Claim / Source / Verification Status / Correction or Note — do this before revealing the model answer.

The important part is not simply identifying "wrong" information — you also need to understand why the claim can't be used as written.

11. PROVE: Verified Research Table

Sarah asks you to prepare a small research table comparing several online learning businesses. Identify claims that matter, locate appropriate sources, check the claims, correct unsupported or incorrect information, and record the verification status.

Fields: Claim, Source, Verification Status (Verified / Not Verified / Conflicting), Correction, Notes. Do not mark something "Verified" simply because AI gave the answer confidently.

12. Human Judgment Checkpoint

What evidence supports this? Does the source actually support the exact claim I'm making? Would this information matter to the client's decision or deliverable? If a claim can't be verified: find another source, qualify the statement, remove it, or tell the client it couldn't be confirmed.

13. Short AI Audit Note

14. Reflection

15. Lesson Close

AI can give you something to investigate. It does not automatically give you something to believe. A professional VA learns to stop at the right moment, check the important claim, correct the output, and only then use the information. AI can speed up the work. Verification protects the work.

4.5 VA Skill: Research synthesis

Build a Client-Ready Research Brief

Big Question How do you turn research into something a client can actually use?

1. Research Is Not Finished When You Find Information

A client usually doesn't need a pile of links, copied notes, or a long explanation of everything you found. They need to understand what you found, what evidence supports it, why it might matter, and what to consider next. That's the job of a research brief. AI can help organize and draft it — you're still responsible for deciding what the evidence actually supports.

2. Finding, Implication, and Recommendation Are Different

Finding — what does the research show? Implication — why might that finding matter to the client? Recommendation — what action could the client consider?

Finding: Three competitor onboarding flows include a short orientation video.

Implication: A short video may be a common way to introduce the learning experience.

Recommendation: BrightPath could consider testing a short orientation video.

The research supports the finding. The implication interprets why it may matter. The recommendation proposes an action. AI can blur these levels if you let it — your job is to keep them separate.

Recommendations are not findingsA recommendation must always be clearly labeled as a recommendation — never folded into the Key Findings or Evidence sections as if the research itself established it. The evidence can support a finding directly; it only ever suggests a recommendation. Readers should never have to guess which one they're looking at.

3. BrightPath Research Request

Sarah asks you to research how other online learning companies handle onboarding, to think about improvements to BrightPath's own experience. You've already developed research questions, gathered information, extracted relevant details, and verified important claims (Lessons 4.1–4.4). Now Sarah needs something she can actually read and use.

4. What a Client-Ready Research Brief Contains

  1. Executive Summary
  2. Key Findings
  3. Evidence
  4. Implications for BrightPath
  5. Recommended Areas to Consider
  6. Limitations / Open Questions

The brief should be concise — the goal isn't to include everything you found, it's to include what Sarah needs to understand the research and decide what deserves further attention.

5. Use Evidence Carefully

For each important finding, ask: what is the source? Does the source actually support this statement? Am I reporting what the source says? Did I turn an observation into a stronger claim? Did I add a recommendation the evidence doesn't justify? If you can't support a statement, don't present it as an established finding — label it an open question, a possible implication, or something requiring further research.

6. AI Can Help With the Brief

AI can organize verified research, group related findings, identify repeated themes, draft concise explanations, suggest a logical structure, and shorten overly long sections. AI should not decide whether a source is trustworthy, whether a claim is supported, whether a recommendation is appropriate, whether a limitation should be disclosed, or what Sarah should ultimately decide.

7. PRACTICE: Finding, Implication, or Recommendation? LEARNING ARTIFACT

8. PRACTICE: Make the Claim Match the Evidence

Source: "Five students reported difficulty locating the orientation video." AI-generated statement: "Students are abandoning onboarding because the orientation video is difficult to find."

The source supports a specific usability problem reported by five students — it does not establish that students are abandoning onboarding. A version that stays within the evidence: "Five students reported difficulty locating the orientation video."

9. PRACTICE: What Belongs in the Brief?

With a larger set of research notes, choose what belongs in the final brief based on relevance to Sarah's request, strength of evidence, usefulness to the client, clarity, and decision relevance. Don't include information simply because it was interesting.

10. PROVE: One-Page Research Brief CAPSTONE EVIDENCE

11. Full AI Audit Note

Because this lesson combines research, verification, synthesis, and client communication, use the more developed audit format.

12. Human Judgment Checkpoint

Can I trace the important findings back to supporting evidence? Did I clearly separate findings, implications, and recommendations? Did I remove claims the research couldn't support? Would Sarah understand what matters without reading all the original research?

13. Reflection

14. Lesson Close

You've now moved from researching information to turning verified information into a client decision aid. A VA who can find information is useful. A VA who can organize, verify, interpret, and communicate information responsibly is much more valuable.
